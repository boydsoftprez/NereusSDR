What is a wideband pan? (explained from zero)

A wideband pan is a different kind of spectrum view from the regular panadapters. Easier to grasp with an analogy, then we'll show what it actually does.

The map analogy

Think of frequency space the same way you think of geographic space.

A regular panadapter is like Google Street View. You see one block in great detail: street signs, house numbers, faces. The view is rich, but narrow — you can only see one block at a time. If you want to see the next block over, you scroll. To see a different city, you have to drive there.

A wideband pan is like opening Google Maps and zooming all the way out to the country view. You see every city at once. You can't read street signs from this view, but you can see where the lights are on, where the traffic is, where activity is concentrated. If you spot something interesting in Boston, you double-click and zoom back down to Street View on that exact block.

Wideband is the country-view of radio. Regular DDC pans are Street View. You use them together.

Why can't you listen to the wideband pan?

Short answer: the wideband data the radio sends is raw from the ADC, before any frequency conversion. To turn it into audio for one specific frequency, the receiver needs to mix it down to baseband and apply a filter — that's what a DDC does. The DDC takes the raw ADC stream and gives you the equivalent of "this 192 kHz window starting at 14.13 MHz, demodulated as USB."

The wideband stream skips that step entirely. The radio just says "here's everything the ADC saw in this 133-microsecond snapshot." There's no center frequency, no filter, no demod. We display it as spectrum. To listen to anything you see in it, you tell a DDC to tune to that frequency — which puts a regular DDC pan + audio on that signal.

Think of it like the difference between a photograph of a busy intersection (wideband — you see everyone at once but can't hear what they're saying) and a microphone pointed at one person (DDC — you hear them clearly but only that one person).

The actual workflow

How operators use wideband + DDC pans together
1. Operator turns on wideband ADC0 — top strip lights up showing all of HF.
2. Operator notices a bright cluster of signals on 17m (where they weren't expecting activity).
3. Operator clicks on that cluster in the wideband strip.
4. The active DDC pan instantly retunes to 18.100 MHz. Now they have a 192 kHz detailed view of that activity, with audio.
5. Operator finds a station they want to chase, fine-tunes within the DDC pan, listens.
6. Meanwhile the wideband strip keeps showing everything else — operator can spot the next opening without losing their current QSO.
